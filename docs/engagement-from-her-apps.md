# What she actually uses — and what it changes

Design input from four apps she finds engaging: YouTube Shorts, Roblox, Pinterest, Block Blast. More useful than anything in the reading list, because it's evidence about her rather than about teenagers.

---

## What the four have in common

1. **No decisions.** All four open straight into the thing. Nothing asks her what she'd like to do.
2. **Short units.** Seconds to minutes, always.
3. **Failure is cheap.** Nothing punishes you. Instant retry, no loss.
4. **Progress is visible** — Block Blast levels, Roblox — **or accumulation is** — Pinterest boards.
5. **Strongly visual.** Three of four.
6. **She makes and curates, not just consumes.** Pinterest boards, Roblox building, and the content she saves is about making things — drawing, nail art, upcycling.

Point 6 is the one our design misses entirely.

---

## 1. Pinterest → she needs something she keeps

**The gap.** Nothing in the app accumulates into something she owns. Streaks are a number, scores are a number, the year map is progress tracking with better graphics. There's nothing she'd browse for pleasure and nothing she'd be sorry to lose.

**The fix: her own explanations, collected.**

At the bottom of a Root Cause run — the moment the thing clicks — the app asks for one line in her own words. Not a form to fill; an offer, skippable, taking fifteen seconds.

> *Say that back in your own words, however you'd explain it to someone.*

Those accumulate into a board she can browse, search and filter by subject. Her words, not ours. Optionally with the graph or the worked example that produced the click.

Why this is the right answer rather than badges:

- **It's genuinely hers.** Pinterest's pull is curation of things you chose, and this is the only thing in the app she'd author.
- **The self-explanation effect is one of the better-evidenced findings in the learning literature.** Putting it in your own words is where understanding consolidates.
- **It's a real revision asset.** In June she has a hand-built set of explanations, in her own language, of exactly the things she once got wrong. Nothing you could buy is worth more than that.
- **It's the Investment leg of the loop** — the app gets more valuable the longer she uses it, in a way that isn't a number.

Let her pin the ones she likes, reorder them, and give the board a title.

**And revise the cosmetics ban.** I said earlier that offering her themes would read as childish. Pinterest says the opposite: she curates aesthetics for pleasure. Let her theme the app — colours, type, a cover image for her board. That's self-expression, not hats, and it's the cheapest ownership signal available.

---

## 2. Block Blast → make getting better legible

**The gap.** Adaptive selection holds her in the 0.3–0.7 mastery band, which is pedagogically correct and completely invisible. Her lived experience is *the questions are about the same difficulty*. The truth is *the questions got harder because you got better*.

Block Blast's entire appeal is that this is visible.

**The fix: a per-subject difficulty tier, shown.**

```
Algebra        ▓▓▓▓▓░░░  Level 5
Geography      ▓▓▓░░░░░  Level 3
```

- Derived from the mean difficulty of items she's currently succeeding at, per subject
- **It only goes up.** A bad week doesn't demote her — that's a Black Hat mechanic and we don't need it
- Levelling up gets a moment: a brief screen, and it goes in the weekly Narrator wrap
- This is not the mastery model exposed. It's one honest number that moves when she genuinely improves

The distinction from v1 matters: v1 showed her a *diagnosis* (subscores, coverage, predicted grade). This shows her an *achievement*. Same underlying data, opposite emotional register.

---

## 3. YouTube Shorts → zero-friction advance (and nothing else)

**Take:** after the verdict, the next question arrives on its own. Two-second beat, then advance, with a tap to linger if she wants to read the solution properly. No "Next" button, no interstitial, no tap between questions.

That's the whole of what Shorts teaches that we should use, and it's a small change with a large difference in feel.

**Don't take the rest.** Infinite feed, no endpoint, no decision to stop — that's exactly what §12 bans, and it's the mechanism doing most of the work in Shorts. The frictionlessness is copyable; the endlessness isn't. The hard stop at ten stays.

---

## 4. Roblox → format variety, and playing *with* people

**Variety.** Roblox isn't a game, it's many short different ones. Twelve MCQs in a row is the same interaction twelve times, and it will get dull however good the questions are.

So the graph engine matters more than it did — not only because it teaches understanding, but because it's a *different thing to do*. Worth adding a third and fourth format in time: ordering/sequencing (strong for history chronology and for multi-step method), and matching pairs.

Target: **no more than three of any one format in a five**.

**Co-op, not only competition.** Roblox is playing *with* friends more than against them. The friends layer is currently all head-to-head — duels and a league. Worth adding a shared goal: a weekly target the group hits together, where her contribution helps rather than beats them. Duolingo's Friends Quest works exactly this way and it consistently outperforms the leaderboard for engagement.

That also softens the thing I'd worry about with a small group of close friends: a purely competitive layer between four girls who see each other every day can curdle. A shared goal can't.

---

## Priority

The first two are the substantial ones and neither is expensive:

| | Effort | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Difficulty tier, visible** | Small — the data exists | Makes improvement legible; the biggest free win available |
| **Her explanations board** | Medium | Fills the ownership gap; doubles as revision material for June |
| Auto-advance | Trivial | Feel |
| Theming | Small | Ownership |
| Format variety | Already planned (graph engine) | — |
| Co-op goal | Session 3, with friends | — |

The board is the one to get right. It's the only thing in the app she'd author, and the only thing she'd miss.
